Assisted Living vs Memory Care vs a Senior Care Home in Tennessee

Assisted living vs memory care vs a senior care home in Tennessee is a choice between licensed settings: assisted living is an Assisted-Care Living Facility, a senior care home is usually a Residential Home for the Aged, and memory care is dementia care inside either. See Levels of Care Explained: What Tennessee Licenses Each As.

Direct answer

In Tennessee, assisted living means a licensed Assisted-Care Living Facility; a smaller senior care home is usually a licensed Residential Home for the Aged; memory care is not a separate license but dementia care delivered inside one of them. All three are licensed by the Tennessee Health Facilities Commission.

Decision questions

Tennessee licenses two main residential settings for older adults. An Assisted-Care Living Facility (ACLF) accepts primarily aged residents for domiciliary care and may provide a higher level of care, including some medical services. A Residential Home for the Aged (RHA) provides room, board and personal services to four or more unrelated residents and is the license most small senior care homes hold. Both are licensed by the Health Facilities Commission; Levels of Care Explained: What Tennessee Licenses Each As walks through each one.

Quick checklist

  • Ask the community which license it holds: ACLF or RHA.
  • Look the license up with the Tennessee Health Facilities Commission before touring twice.
  • Ask what services the license lets the community provide on site.
  • Ask what happens if your parent's needs exceed what that license allows.

Red flags

  • The community cannot tell you its license type
  • "Assisted living" in the name but an RHA license, with no explanation of the difference
  • Promises of nursing care the license does not cover

No. Tennessee does not issue a separate memory care license. Memory care is dementia care delivered inside a licensed ACLF or RHA, often in a secured unit, and the facility must meet the dementia-care requirements in its licensure rules. So the questions that matter are the community's license, how its dementia unit is secured and staffed, and what training its staff have. Memory Care: The Signs It Is Time covers the signs that a move is due.

Quick checklist

  • Ask whether the memory care area is a secured unit and how exits are controlled.
  • Ask how many staff are on the unit on day and night shifts.
  • Ask what dementia training staff receive and how often.
  • Ask what behaviors would lead the community to ask your parent to move out.

Red flags

  • A "memory care" label with no secured area or dementia training to show
  • No clear answer on night staffing
  • Move-out criteria that are not in writing

Start from what your parent needs help with. If they need help with bathing, dressing, medications or meals but are safe in an open setting, assisted living fits. If dementia causes wandering, exit-seeking or unsafe judgment, look at memory care. If they would do better in a quiet home with a handful of residents and mostly need supervision and personal care, a licensed residential home for the aged can fit. Activities of Daily Living: How Communities Assess Care Needs helps you score those needs.

Quick checklist

  • List the daily activities your parent needs help with today.
  • Note any wandering, falls or night-time confusion in the last three months.
  • Ask your parent's doctor which setting they recommend and why.
  • Tour at least one community of each type before deciding.

Red flags

  • A community that accepts your parent without an assessment
  • A care level chosen on price alone
  • A setting that cannot manage the needs your parent already has

The Health Facilities Commission inspects licensed facilities and acts on complaints. Ask the community for its most recent survey and any plan of correction, and check the Commission's published records yourself. Look for repeat citations in the same area, such as medication administration or staffing, rather than a single minor finding. How to Read a Tennessee Assisted Living Inspection and Licensure Record explains what the findings mean.

Quick checklist

  • Ask the community for its latest survey and plan of correction.
  • Check for repeat findings in the same area.
  • Ask what changed after each finding.
  • Note the date of the last inspection.

Red flags

  • The community will not share its latest survey
  • Repeat findings on medication handling
  • A plan of correction that was never completed

In Shelby County you will find all three: larger ACLFs with memory care wings, stand-alone memory care communities and small residential homes. Compare by license, care level and monthly cost in the same city, Germantown, Collierville or Bartlett for example, then shortlist two or three to tour. Families in DeSoto County, Mississippi, and Crittenden County, Arkansas, deal with a different state licensing body, so compare within one state first.

Quick checklist

  • Pick one city to compare first.
  • Shortlist two or three communities per care type.
  • Bring the same question list to every tour.
  • Get each community's monthly price in writing.

Red flags

  • Comparing a Tennessee ACLF with a Mississippi personal care home as if the rules were the same
  • Choosing before touring in person
  • A shortlist built from paid placement only
