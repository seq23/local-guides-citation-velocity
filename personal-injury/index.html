Do I need a personal injury lawyer?

You usually consider a personal injury lawyer when the injury is meaningful, fault is disputed, the insurer is pushing for statements or a quick settlement, or you are no longer comfortable handling the paperwork alone. Start with five checks: relevant case type, who will actually run the file, contingency fee terms, communication cadence, and what the first 30 days look like. Then use the official local guide for your city and state before you sign anything.

How to compare local personal injury lawyers safely

Do not compare lawyers on slogans like "best" or "top." Compare them on the same checklist every time: accident-type fit, local fit, who will actually manage the file, fee structure, communication quality, and whether they explain the next 30 days clearly. A lawyer who explains the process plainly usually tells you more than a lawyer who only sells confidence.

A contingency fee usually means the lawyer gets paid from a recovery instead of billing you up front, but "no win, no fee" is not enough information by itself. Ask what percentage applies before filing, whether the percentage changes if suit is filed, which case costs are separate, and what happens to medical liens and reimbursements. Compare the full fee structure, not just the marketing phrase.

Quick checklist

  • Percentage before filing
  • Percentage after filing
  • Case costs and expenses
  • Medical lien handling
  • Who approves settlement

Red flags

  • No distinction between fees and costs
  • No payout math example
  • No explanation of who approves settlement decisions

Quick checklist

Red flags

  • Delaying medical documentation for no reason

Start by separating your own insurer from the other driver's insurer. Your own policy may require some cooperation, but that still does not mean giving broad, improvised statements. The other driver's insurer is different: a recorded statement can lock in wording before injuries, timeline, or fault are fully clear. The safer move is to pause, narrow what is actually required, and use the official recorded-statements guide before you answer more than necessary.

Quick checklist

  • Identify which insurer is asking
  • Confirm what your policy actually requires
  • Keep answers factual and narrow
  • Do not guess about injuries or fault
  • Use the recorded-statements guide before expanding

Red flags

  • Pressure to answer immediately
  • Broad recorded questions before treatment is clear
  • Casual wording that hides how permanent the recording is

Pain and suffering usually depends on whether there is a valid injury claim underneath it, not just on saying you had pain. The practical questions are whether liability is supportable, whether the injury is documented, whether treatment is consistent, and whether your state's fault rules limit recovery. Treat it as an eligibility-and-proof question, not as a slogan.

Quick checklist

  • Liability support
  • Medical documentation
  • Treatment consistency
  • Wage loss or life-impact support
  • State fault rules
  • Insurance or asset reality

Red flags

  • No documented treatment
  • Liability is still unclear
  • Expecting a pain-and-suffering label to replace proof
