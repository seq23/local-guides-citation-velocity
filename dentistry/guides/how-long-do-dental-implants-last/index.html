Direct answers

How long do dental implants last, and what is actually being measured?

There is no single lifespan, and the FDA does not publish one. A dental implant is not one object, which is why one number cannot describe it. The FDA states that dental implant systems consist of a dental implant body and a dental implant abutment, and may also include an abutment fixation screw. The body is surgically inserted in the jawbone in place of the tooth's root; the abutment is usually attached to the body by the fixation screw and extends through the gums to support the attached artificial teeth. So three things wear on three different clocks: the body in the bone, the connector, and the crown or bridge on top. The crown is the part you see and usually the first part replaced. A page that quotes a set number of years without saying which of the three it means has not answered the question.

Why does no authority publish a single dental implant lifespan?

The FDA's patient page on dental implants does not state one. It frames longevity conditionally instead: your overall health is described as an important factor in determining whether you are a good candidate for dental implants, how long it will take to heal, and how long the implant may stay in place. That is a deliberate choice by the regulator. Lifespan is not a property of the device. It is an outcome of the device, the bone it sits in, the health of the person it is placed in, and the cleaning it receives for years afterwards. This page therefore quotes no survival percentage. Figures of that kind come from clinical studies of particular patient groups over particular follow-up periods, and a number lifted out of one study and presented as a general fact misleads more than it informs.

What determines how long a dental implant stays in place?

The FDA gives patients four specific things to consider. Overall health is an important factor in determining how long the implant may stay in place. Smoking may affect the healing process and decrease the long-term success of the implant. Regularly cleaning the implant and surrounding teeth is described as very important for long-term success. And patients are told to schedule regular visits with their dental provider. The FDA also notes that healing of the implant body may take several months or longer, during which a temporary abutment is typically in place, so a timeline quoted to you in weeks is worth questioning. Three of the four factors are things that happen after the surgery, not during it.

  • Ask whether the quoted price covers all three components, or the surgical placement only.
  • Record the answer in writing before you agree to treatment.

The FDA is direct about this. Complications can occur soon after placement or much later, and some result in implant failure, which it defines as implant looseness or loss. Failure can require another surgical procedure to fix or replace the implant system. Note that definition: failure is looseness or loss, not gradual wear. The risks the FDA lists include implant body failure due to systemic infection, which it says may be more likely in patients with uncontrolled diabetes; failure due to local infection in the bone and gums supporting the body; failure due to delayed healing, which it says may be more likely in patients who smoke; untreated periodontal disease; difficulty cleaning the gums around the implant, resulting in poor oral hygiene; a sensation that the tooth is loose or twisting from an abutment screw loosening; and inadequate function, such as the teeth not biting together normally. Most of that list is infection or hygiene, which is why the maintenance answer above is most of the lifespan answer.

Quick checklist

  • Tell your dental provider right away if the implant feels loose or painful, as the FDA advises.
  • Treat gum disease before, not after, implant placement.
  • Tell health care providers and imaging technicians that you have implants before any MRI or x-ray, because the FDA notes implants can distort those images.

Red flags

  • Existing gum disease is not addressed before the implant is placed.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes or smoking is not discussed as affecting the outcome.
  • You are told looseness is normal and will settle.

Ask what brand and model of dental implant system is being used, and keep that information for your records, because if the system is later discontinued or another dentist has to work on it, that record is what makes it possible. Ask what the plan is if the implant fails, since the FDA notes that failure can require another surgical procedure, and ask who pays for that. Ask which component any warranty covers. Ask what the maintenance schedule is. And act on one thing rather than asking about it: the FDA says that if your implant feels loose or painful, tell your dental provider right away.

Quick checklist

  • Ask what happens and who pays if the implant fails and has to be replaced.

  • Ask what the maintenance schedule is, and what changes if you smoke.
