Important context

At what age should my child first see a dentist and what should I expect

By the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing — whichever comes first. This is the standard recommendation from the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry and the American Dental Association. The first visit is short and mostly about establishing a baseline: a quick look at the erupting teeth, a discussion of feeding, bottle and pacifier habits, brushing and fluoride, and a plan for follow-up. Expect a conversation with the parent more than a procedure on the child.

Timeline table

What happens at each stage, and what to ask

Visits change substantially as a child grows. Knowing the stage tells you what the appointment should actually cover.

StageWhat the visit typically coversWhat to ask
First visit — by age 1Brief exam on the parent's lap, review of feeding and bottle habits, brushing demonstration, fluoride discussionWhat should I be doing differently at home right now?
Toddler — ages 1 to 3Monitoring eruption, checking for early decay, habit discussion including pacifier and thumbAre there early signs of decay I should watch for?
Preschool — ages 3 to 6Child sits in the chair alone, cleaning, possible first X-rays based on risk, sealant discussion as molars eruptDoes my child need X-rays yet, and why now?
School age — 6 to 12Permanent molars erupting, sealants, spacing and alignment assessment, independent brushing checkAre the permanent molars sealed, and is spacing developing normally?
AdolescentWisdom-tooth monitoring, orthodontic follow-up, dietary and sports-guard discussionDo the wisdom teeth need monitoring or action?
Checklist

How to prepare a young child for a first dental visit

Most of the difficulty at a first visit comes from the build-up, not the appointment.

  • Book a morning slot when your child is rested rather than after a full day.
  • Keep the description simple and neutral — the dentist will count the teeth.
  • Avoid words your child has no reason to fear yet: hurt, pain, needle, drill, shot.
  • Do not promise it will not hurt. Promise you will stay with them.
  • Practise at home by letting your child lie back while you look at their teeth.
  • Expect to hold your child on your lap during the exam — this is normal at this age.
  • If your own dental anxiety is high, consider having another adult take them.
